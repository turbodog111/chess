Queen's Gambit Declined

Black declines the pawn and supports d5 with ...e6. The structure is one of the oldest in chess. White's main tries are Bg5 systems, the Exchange minority attack, and the more active Ragozin and Tarrasch.

Orthodox

D60–D69

1. d4 d5 2. c4 e6 3. Nc3 Nf6 4. Bg5 Be7 5. e3 O-O 6. Nf3 Nbd7

The classical QGD. Black is solid, the light-squared bishop is still in the box, and the centre will be resolved by ...dxc4 or ...c6. Capablanca's releasing manoeuvre ...dxc4 and ...Nd5 is the historic main line.

White

  • Rc1, Bd3, and pressure the c-file after ...dxc4.
  • Keep a grip on e4 so Black cannot free with ...e5.

Black

  • Play ...c6, take on c4, then ...Nd5 to swap a pair of pieces.
  • Do not sit still: the bad bishop on c8 needs a route via b7 or d7.
  • 7.Rc1 c6 8.Bd3 dxc4 9.Bxc4 Nd5Capablanca's freeing manoeuvre, the old main line.
  • 7.Qc2Rubinstein. White delays Rc1 and watches e4.
  • 7.Bd3Allows ...dxc4 with tempo. Still fully playable.

Tartakower

D58–D59

1. d4 d5 2. c4 e6 3. Nc3 Nf6 4. Bg5 Be7 5. e3 O-O 6. Nf3 h6 7. Bh4 b6

Black asks the bishop a question with ...h6, then fianchettos. The c8-bishop finally becomes a piece. This has been a world-championship workhorse because it is solid and still has bite.

White

  • Take on f6 only if the structure after ...Bxf6 favours a bind.
  • Play Rc1, Bd3, and cxd5, aiming at hanging pawns on c5 and d5.

Black

  • Develop ...Bb7 and ...Nbd7, then choose ...c5 or ...Ne4.
  • The hanging-pawn structure is a feature, not a bug, if the pieces are active.
  • 8.Be2 Bb7 9.Bxf6 Bxf6 10.cxd5 exd5A main tabiya. Black accepts an IQP or hanging pawns.
  • 8.cxd5 Nxd5The other recapture, keeping a compact centre.
  • 8.Bxf6 Bxf6 9.cxd5 exd5Immediate trade. Black's bishop pair compensates.

Lasker

D56–D57

1. d4 d5 2. c4 e6 3. Nc3 Nf6 4. Bg5 Be7 5. e3 O-O 6. Nf3 h6 7. Bh4 Ne4 8. Bxe7 Qxe7

Black forces a pair of exchanges with ...Ne4. The resulting position is one of the most reliable equalising methods in the QGD: fewer pieces, a sound structure, and a clear plan of ...c6, ...Nxc3, and ...dxc4.

White

  • Keep enough pieces on the board to make the space count.
  • Qc2, Rc1, and Bd3; do not allow a total liquidation.

Black

  • Trade on c3, take on c4, and play ...c5 or ...e5 later.
  • The queen on e7 supports both central breaks.
  • 9.cxd5 Nxc3 10.bxc3 exd5Exchange structure. White has the c-file; Black is very solid.
  • 9.Qc2 Nxc3 10.Qxc3Keeps more tension. Black continues ...c6 or ...dxc4.
  • 9.Rc1A useful waiting move before deciding the centre.

Exchange

D35–D36

1. d4 d5 2. c4 e6 3. Nc3 Nf6 4. cxd5 exd5 5. Bg5 c6 6. Qc2

White fixes a Carlsbad structure and aims for the minority attack b4-b5. Black can also be attacked in the centre with Nge2 and f3-e4. This is one of White's most practical tries against the QGD.

White

  • Minority attack: Rb1, b4, b5, and a weakness on c6.
  • Or Nge2, f3, e4 if Black commits to a kingside fianchetto.

Black

  • Meet b4-b5 with ...a5, ...b5, or a knight on d6.
  • Kingside play with ...Ne4, ...f5, and ...Bd6 is the other plan.
  • 6.e3 Bf5The old line. Qc2 is designed to stop this bishop sortie.
  • 5.Bf4Modern Exchange. White avoids Bg5 and keeps the bishop pair options.
  • 6...g6 7.e3 Bf5Black develops the bishop anyway and fianchettos later.

Cambridge Springs

D52

1. d4 d5 2. c4 e6 3. Nc3 Nf6 4. Bg5 Nbd7 5. e3 c6 6. Nf3 Qa5

Black leaves the f8-bishop at home and pins Nc3 with ...Qa5. The tactical point is ...Bb4 and ...Ne4, hitting c3 and g5. Named for the 1904 tournament, it is still a sound surprise weapon.

White

  • Nd2 unpins and covers e4. Then a3 or cxd5.
  • Do not grab on d5 too early if ...Bb4 and ...Ne4 are both in.

Black

  • Play ...Bb4, ...Ne4, and sometimes ...dxc4.
  • If White is careless, the bishop on g5 hangs after ...Ne4.
  • 7.Nd2 Bb4 8.Qc2Main line. White overprotects c3.
  • 7.cxd5 Nxd5Opens the queen's pin. Tactics on c3 follow.
  • 7.Bxf6 Nxf6 8.Bd3Gives up the bishop pair to kill the ...Ne4 idea.

Ragozin

D38

1. d4 d5 2. c4 e6 3. Nc3 Nf6 4. Nf3 Bb4 5. cxd5 exd5 6. Bg5 h6

Black pins the knight with ...Bb4 instead of the meek ...Be7. The line is more active than the Orthodox and is a favourite of players who want a fight from a QGD move order.

White

  • Qa4+ or e3 and Bd3, forcing Black to decide the bishop.
  • Take on f6 and double Black's pawns if the king stays in the centre.

Black

  • Castle, then ...c5 or ...Nbd7-b6.
  • Keep the pin until White spends time with a3.
  • 5.Bg5 dxc4Vienna mix. Black takes on c4 after the pin is in.
  • 5.e3 O-O 6.Bd3A quieter Ragozin, often transposing to a Nimzo structure.
  • 6.Bh4Keeps the pin. Black continues ...c5 or ...Nbd7.

Tarrasch

D32–D34

1. d4 d5 2. c4 e6 3. Nc3 c5 4. cxd5 exd5 5. Nf3 Nc6 6. g3 Nf6 7. Bg2

Black hits back with ...c5 and accepts an isolated queen's pawn. The pieces get free play; the pawn on d5 is the long-term bill. The Rubinstein system with g3 is White's most respected answer.

White

  • Blockade d4, pressure d5, and trade into a favourable ending.
  • Bg2, O-O, and often Bg5 or Na4-c5.

Black

  • Activity first: ...Be7, ...O-O, ...Re8, and ...Bg4.
  • The IQP is a weapon while pieces remain.
  • 6.g3 Nf6 7.Bg2 Be7 8.O-O O-OMain Rubinstein Tarrasch.
  • 4.e3 Nf6 5.Nf3 Nc6Symmetrical Tarrasch; less isolated-pawn play.
  • 3...Nf6 4.Nf3 c5Semi-Tarrasch, usually with ...cxd4 rather than an IQP.
